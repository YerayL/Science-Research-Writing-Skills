# How to Write the Discussion

## Core Idea
The Discussion widens from your specific results back to the broader context. It maps your findings onto the existing literature, explores implications, and identifies the achievement and contribution of your study. It is the mirror image of the Introduction.

## The Generic Discussion Model

```
1. ANNOUNCE THE STRUCTURE OR CONTENT OF THE DISCUSSION SECTION

2. STATE THE ACHIEVEMENT/CONTRIBUTION OF THE STUDY*
   ± REVISIT BACKGROUND INFORMATION/LITERATURE TO 'REBOOT' READER
   ± REVISIT GAP/AIM/METHOD
   ± REVISIT RESULTS AND EXPLORE THEIR IMPLICATIONS
   ± MAP TO LITERATURE/KNOWLEDGE FOR COMPARISON/SUPPORT
   ± IDENTIFY POTENTIAL LIMITATIONS AND SUGGESTIONS FOR FUTURE WORK

3. RESTATE THE ACHIEVEMENT/CONTRIBUTION/IMPACT OF THE STUDY
   ± IDENTIFY POTENTIAL APPLICATIONS

*The ACHIEVEMENT/CONTRIBUTION is the most free-flowing component — it may
appear at the start, end, or throughout the Discussion.
```

## Key Concepts

### Achievement vs. Contribution
- **Achievement**: Internal to the study — did you resolve the specific research question? (e.g. "We identified X")
- **Contribution**: Outward-facing — how does the study affect knowledge, research, or the real world?
- **Use the same verb** from the Introduction's aim statement to track achievement clearly for the reader (don't switch to a "synonym")

### Introduction-Discussion Symmetry
| Introduction | Discussion |
|-------------|------------|
| Start wide, narrow down | Start narrow (from your results), widen out |
| Present past/current research | Show how your study contributes to that research |
| Identify a gap/problem | Discuss to what extent you resolved it |
| Enable reader to enter the central section | Enable reader to exit the paper |

## Language & Writing Skills: Modal Verbs
- **may/might/could** → possibility, speculation, lower certainty
- **should** → expectation, recommendation based on evidence
- **must** → logical necessity, strong inference
- **would** → conditional, hypothetical

## Anti-patterns
- **Repeating the Results section verbatim**: The Discussion interprets; it doesn't re-present
- **Ignoring contradictory evidence**: Discuss results that don't fit your hypothesis — it builds credibility
- **Over-speculating**: Modal verbs should match evidence strength; don't use *may* for findings you can prove
- **Missing the achievement statement**: The reader needs to know explicitly what the study accomplished
- **New results in Discussion**: Don't introduce findings not presented in Results

## Key Takeaways
1. Map your results to the literature — comparison and support are essential Discussion functions
2. State the achievement explicitly, using the same verb as in the Introduction's aim statement
3. Include limitations honestly — they don't weaken the paper; they build credibility
4. Use modal verbs precisely: *may* (possible), *should* (expected), *must* (logically necessary)
5. The Discussion should answer: "To what extent did we resolve the problem stated in the Introduction?"
6. End with contribution/impact/applications — this is the "get out" that mirrors the Introduction's "get in"

## Connects To
- **Writing the Introduction**: Symmetrical structure — narrows vs. widens, gap vs. resolution
- **Writing about Results**: Discussion explores implications of results more deeply
- **Writing the Conclusion**: Conclusion distills the Discussion's key messages
