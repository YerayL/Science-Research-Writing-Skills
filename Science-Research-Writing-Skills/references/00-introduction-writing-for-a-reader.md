# Introduction: Writing for a Reader — Wrapping Information in a Narrative

## Core Idea
Science research writing is not about presenting information — it's about wrapping information in a narrative scaffold so the reader cannot fail to understand. The aim is not simply to make it possible for the reader to understand; the aim is to make it **impossible for the reader NOT to understand**.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Reverse-Engineering Approach**: Deconstruct successful published papers in your target field to build writing models. Instead of being told *what* to do, you discover *how* to do it by analysing the organisational scaffold, writing patterns, and language of well-written articles.
  - **When to use**: Before writing any section of a paper, or when you need to adapt to a new journal or genre.
  - **How**: (1) Build sentence/paragraph-based models identifying functions, (2) Mine successful texts for vocabulary, (3) Identify and master relevant grammar/language/writing skills, (4) Continually reinforce via reading in the field.

- **Narrative Wrap**: The communication scaffold that surrounds information and makes it intelligible. It includes titles, subheadings, paragraph function, graphics order, commentary on data, verb tense choices, and paragraph-entry sentences.
  - **When to use**: Every time you write. Information alone is neutral — the narrative wrap gives it function and direction.

- **The Two-Layer Model of Science Writing**:
  - **Planning: The Underlying Narrative Scaffold** — identifying genre/readership, dividing into subsections, choosing graphics order/position, planning paragraph function/order, planning sentence function/order within paragraphs.
  - **Writing: The Active Narrative Wrap** — commenting on information for the reader, identifying function of data, making grammar choices that communicate function, making vocabulary choices that help the reader, starting paragraphs/sentences in reader-friendly ways, creating helpful titles/subtitles.

## Key Concepts
- **IMRD Structure**: Introduction → Methods → Results → Discussion (the traditional shape, though loosening in modern publishing)
- **Magnetic South**: The destination point of the text — everything should lead consistently toward it
- **Genre-Based Writing**: Different text types (research article, grant proposal, conference abstract, review article) require different structures
- **Information-Surfing**: Modern readers scroll titles/abstracts rapidly; your paper competes for attention online
- **Reverse Engineering**: Active reading strategy — stepping back from information to understand *how* it is delivered

## Mental Models
- Think of writing as **Painting by Numbers**: you don't need to be da Vinci — analysing the organisational scaffold of good papers delivers a strategy for producing a credible article
- Think of a paper as an **hourglass shape**: Introduction narrows toward the central report section; Discussion widens after it
- Think of the reader as someone who needs to be **shown the function** of every piece of data, not just presented with it
- "Academic English is no-one's first language" — native and non-native speakers face the same writing challenges

## Key Takeaways
1. Always reverse-engineer target articles before writing — this is your evidence-based guide
2. Build models that describe sentence/paragraph **functions** (not content) so they're transferable
3. Plan the narrative scaffold BEFORE typing sentences — step away from the keyboard
4. Every sentence should have an identifiable function clear to the reader
5. The 4-step strategy is a lifelong tool: build models → mine vocabulary → master grammar → continually update via reading
