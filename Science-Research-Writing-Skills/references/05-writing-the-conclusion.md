# How to Write the Conclusion

## Core Idea
The Conclusion section (sometimes called Conclusions) distills the study into a self-contained take-home message. It should function as an independent mini-document, as many readers go directly from Abstract to Conclusion without reading the full paper.

## The Generic Conclusions Model (11 Components)

```
1.  WHAT IS IN THE PAPER
2.  WHAT THE PAPER/STUDY HAS ACHIEVED
3.  RELEVANT BACKGROUND INFORMATION
4.  THE GAP/AIM/NEED FOR THE STUDY
5.  THE METHOD/APPROACH
6.  KEY RESULTS WITH EVALUATIVE COMMENTS
7.  IMPLICATIONS OF THE RESULTS
8.  POTENTIAL OR ACTUAL LIMITATIONS
9.  POTENTIAL OR ACTUAL APPLICATIONS
10. HOW THE STUDY ADVANCES KNOWLEDGE
11. POTENTIAL FUTURE DIRECTIONS FOR RESEARCH
```

**Key usage note**: Map these 11 components onto your target articles to see typical order and proportion. Not all components appear in every Conclusion.

## Anti-patterns
- **Copy-pasting the Abstract**: The Conclusion serves a different purpose — it focuses on what was achieved and what it means going forward
- **Introducing completely new information**: The Conclusion synthesises; it doesn't debut new findings
- **Weak finish**: Ending on a vague note undermines the impact of the entire paper

## Key Takeaways
1. The Conclusion should work as a standalone document for readers who skip the full paper
2. Always include an explicit achievement statement — what did this study accomplish?
3. Include future directions — they show the work opens new possibilities, not closes a door
4. Map target article Conclusions to see which of the 11 components they use and in what order
5. Be specific about contribution and impact — avoid generic "this work is important" statements
