# How to Write about Results

## Core Idea
The Results section is where you present findings AND comment on them — not just a data dump. The narrative wrap is critical here: readers need to know what the data means, not just what it says.

## The Generic Results Model

```
1. REVISITING/SUMMARISING THE METHOD
   + GENERAL STATEMENT ABOUT THE RESULTS
   + INVITATION TO VIEW GRAPHIC + CONTENT OF GRAPHIC

2. SPECIFIC/KEY RESULTS ± EVALUATIVE LANGUAGE/COMMENTS
   + COMPARISON WITH RESULTS IN OTHER STUDIES / 'HAPPY' WORDS
   + COMPARISON WITH MODEL/SIMULATION/PREDICTED RESULTS
   + EXPLANATION OF RESULTS VIA KNOWN FACTS/METHOD DETAILS

3. PROBLEMS/ISSUES WITH RESULTS ± REASONS

4. POSSIBLE IMPLICATIONS OF RESULTS / 'HAPPY' WORDS
```

### Verb Tense in the Results Section
- **Present Simple**: Description of what can be seen in a graphic; background facts that explain results (material X *is* able to…); established truths
- **Past Simple**: How the information in the graphic was obtained; what the authors found (X occurred)
- **Tense as confidence signal**: Past Simple reports findings tentatively; Present Simple reflects a belief that findings are permanent truths. Check your target articles for convention.

## The Certainty Continuum

A critical framework for communicating your level of confidence in causal relationships:

### Choose verbs that accurately reflect causality:
| Strength | Verbs |
|----------|-------|
| **Direct/strong causal** | cause, produce, be due to |
| **Partial cause** | contribute to, be a factor in |
| **Causal process implied** | lead to, give rise to |
| **Initial cause in chain** | originate in, arise from |
| **Product rather than effect** | produce, yield, generate |
| **Indirect/weak/implicit** | be related to, be linked to, be associated with |

### Key distinction:
- **X produced Y** → X = cause, Y = effect (directional)
- **X is linked to Y** → no direction specified, may not even be causal
- **X originated in Y** → X = effect, Y = cause (reverse direction)

## Anti-patterns
- **Naked numbers**: Presenting data without evaluative comments (e.g. "43%" vs "only 43%" or "as high as 43%")
- **Overclaiming**: Using strong causal verbs when the evidence only supports correlation
- **Underclaiming**: Using weak language when results clearly demonstrate causation
- **Graphic dumping**: Including a figure without inviting the reader to view it and commenting on what it shows

## Key Takeaways
1. Always include evaluative comments with data — show the reader what the numbers mean
2. Use the Certainty Continuum consciously: match verb strength to your actual evidence level
3. Invite the reader to view graphics explicitly, then comment on what they show
4. Past Simple = tentative finding; Present Simple = established truth — this choice communicates confidence
5. Compare your results to other studies and to models/predictions — this is part of the Results narrative
6. Problems/issues with results should be stated honestly — builds credibility

## Connects To
- **Writing the Discussion**: Results provide the foundation; Discussion explores implications more deeply
- **Writing the Introduction**: Verb tense choices carry the same meaning as in the Introduction
- **Checklist and Tips**: Avoiding naked numbers and maintaining narrative
