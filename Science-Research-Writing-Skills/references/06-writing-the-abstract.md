# Writing the Abstract

## Core Idea
The Abstract is a **high-stakes standalone document**. More people will read the Abstract than the whole paper. It must function independently, engage readers scrolling through titles/abstracts online, and communicate the value of the study quickly and unmistakably.

## Guidelines

### Clarity & Coherence
- **Explicitly identify your own contribution**: Your familiarity with the study makes it harder to see potential ambiguity. A Present Simple statement may sound like established knowledge when it's actually your finding.
- **Combine sentence functions**: In a limited word count, a single sentence can establish significance AND identify a gap.
- **Review linking strategies** from Writing the Introduction to ensure smooth flow.

### Timing
- Write the Abstract **last** — it derives from the paper, not the other way around
- Writing it too early risks: (1) content that doesn't match the final paper, (2) missing key findings
- Content, style, and length depend on where you plan to publish

## Types of Abstract
1. **Simple/Standard Abstracts** — single paragraph, no subheadings
2. **Structured Abstracts** — with headings (Background/Aims, Methods, Results, Conclusions)
3. **Abstracts with Significance Statement** — accompanied by a separate statement of impact
4. **Graphical Abstracts** — visual summary alongside text

## The Generic Abstract Model

```
1. SIGNIFICANCE OF THE TOPIC / ESSENTIAL FACTUAL BACKGROUND
   THE CHALLENGE/PROBLEM
   WHAT THE PAPER/STUDY DOES (may include ACHIEVEMENT/VALUE + 'happy' words)

2. METHOD/MATERIALS + 'happy' words
   RESULTS + 'happy' words / COMPARISONS WITH EXISTING RESULTS + 'happy' words
   IMPLICATIONS

3. MAPPING TO EXISTING KNOWLEDGE + 'happy' words
   ACHIEVEMENT/VALUE/CONTRIBUTION + 'happy' words
   APPLICATIONS + 'happy' words
```

**Note the multiple locations for 'happy' words** — the Abstract is where you actively sell the value of your work.

### Verb Tense in the Abstract
- **What the paper does**: Present Simple (this paper presents, we describe)
- **Method (what was done)**: Past Simple
- **Results & Implications**: Past Simple or Present Simple (Present Simple engages attention more immediately)
- **Achievement/Value/Contribution**: Present Perfect (have shown) or Present Simple

## Anti-patterns
- **Writing the Abstract first**: It will likely not match the final paper
- **Ambiguous ownership**: Reader can't tell if "X suggests Y" is your finding or established knowledge
- **Missing 'happy' words**: The Abstract must actively communicate value — don't be modest
- **Too much background, too little contribution**: Balance background with the unique value of your study

## Key Takeaways
1. Always write the Abstract last — it derives from the paper
2. Use multiple 'happy' words at each model component position to sell your study
3. Present Simple tense for results in the Abstract engages readers more immediately
4. Ensure the Abstract works for readers who never read the full paper
5. Reverse-engineer Abstracts from your target journal — conventions vary significantly

## Connects To
- **Writing the Introduction**: Similar structure; Introduction is the long form
- **Writing the Conclusion**: Abstract and Conclusion both summarise, but Abstract is the entry, Conclusion the exit
- **Writing the Title**: Title and Abstract work together for discoverability
